# The Value of Science — Richard Feynman

I always wondered about the place and need of science in this so-called troubled world. This piece by Feynman talks exactly about that.

Social problems are very much harder than scientific ones. A scientist looking at non-scientific problems is just as dumb as the next guy.

To every man is given the key to the gates of heaven; the same key opens the gates of hell. What is the value of the key? If the instructions aren't clear then it's a very dangerous object to use — but you can't enter heaven without the key. Quite the dilemma. Also, without the key, the instructions would be of no value.

All this considered, there is a value of science that is — it can produce something.

The value of society in a way is to arrange things so people can enjoy their curiosity. Therein lies a value that is intellectual enjoyment.

The underappreciated worldview science provides is far greater than the imagination of man. Imagine the scale, and imagine science fiction — there's so much more than vampires and angels.

The use of an idea and the idea itself. This is a debate I had with my roommate sometime ago. It was about intent and output. I have the understanding that good intention is the defining factor, but the opposing view was that it doesn't matter if there isn't a tangible output.

The main outlook of the paper is Feynman's focus on the value of science that promotes discovery without expectations. The constant tussle of being right is what the world works on, but a student of science is taught to be doubtful. Scientific knowledge is a body of work with varying degrees of certainty. Ignorance, uncertainty, and doubt are the defining characteristics of a student of science.

The hopes for the future today are, in many ways, those of yesterday. We have a better understanding than people before us, but what we are still capable of feels gigantic in comparison to what we've accomplished. Why is that?

We come back to the key analogy — even with greater knowledge we don't seem to have clear instructions on how to use it.

Men across time have tried to state a meaning for life: a one-liner, a proverb, a culture, a religion — all in order to give direction or meaning to our actions. The proponents of one answer have looked with horror at the actions of the believers in another. History is created by false beliefs, the showcase of the apparent infinite and wondrous capacities of humans. The dream is to find the open channel.

So what is the meaning of life? What can be the explanation of the apparent mystery of the world? Of all the knowledge humankind has amassed — be it the ancients or modern science — the frank answer is: we do not know. This is the open channel Feynman talks about. Accepting that we don't know. This is the philosophy that led to the formation of democracy: discovery, not design.

Doubt and discuss. We can't suppress discussions and criticism by saying this is the end, that we are part of the "developed" now. "Of all its many values, the greatest must be the freedom to doubt."

*I . . . a universe of atoms . . . an atom in the universe.*
